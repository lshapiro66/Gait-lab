Why is the study of primate gait important?

Primates utilize a walking gait pattern that differs from most other groups of mammals. Primates prefer a diagonal sequence/diagonal couplets gait when walking quadrupedally, in contrast to the lateral sequence gait used by almost all other mammals.

The evolution of the primate gait may be related to the environment in which the first primates evolved and therefore may tell us something about primate origins. It is also probable that human bipedalism has its functional antecedents in diagonal sequence walking.

Lateral Sequence and Diagonal Sequence Gait

In a lateral sequence walk the footfall of a hindlimb is followed by the footfall of the forelimb on the same side (ipsilateral). To properly identify the gait pattern begin by noting hindlimb contact. If sequential contact is made by the same side forelimb, such as right hindlimb followed by right forelimb (RH,RF), and the next sequence is left hindlimb followed by left (ipsilateral) forelimb (LH,LF), the gait is a lateral sequence (LS) walk.

In a diagonal sequence walk the footfall of a hindlimb is followed by that of a diagonally opposite (contralateral) forelimb. Begin by noting hindlimb contact. If sequential contact is made by the opposite (contralateral) forelimb, such as right hindlimb followed by left forelimb (RH,LF), and if the next sequence is left hindlimb followed by right (contralateral) forelimb (LH,RF), the gait is a diagonal sequence (DS) walk.

Running: Trot and Pace

The trot is a symmetrical gait generated by diagonal pairs of limbs which move almost simultaneously. As a result, feet at diagonal ends of the body strike the ground together. Animals with short torsos and long legs have difficulty trotting, since their hindlimbs interfere with their forelimbs.

The pace is a symmetrical gait in which support is maintained by lateral pairs of limbs. Forelimb and hindlimb on the same side of the body move back and forth synchronously generating a rolling motion. During a pace, the animal moves by swinging the forelimb and hindlimb pair on one side while bearing weight on the other lateral pair. The pace is used by long-legged animals. It allows them to travel without interference between front and hind limbs, as might occur during a trot.

Gallop

The gallop is an asymmetrical gait used for high-speed locomotion, and includes a period in which all four limbs are suspended in the air.

During a gallop the second footfall of the forelimb pair and the second footfall of the hindlimb pair are lead limbs, that is, although they fall second in timing to the other limb, they are positioned farther ahead of the first footfall, therefore, they lead.

There are two patterns of gallop: the transverse gallop and the rotary gallop. In a transverse gallop, both leading limbs are on the same side of the body. An example of a sequence of footfalls for a transverse gallop would be LHRHLFRF. In a rotary gallop, the lead forelimb is on one side of the body while the lead hindlimb is on the other side of the body. An example of a rotary gallop would be RHLHLFRF.

Recap

We have defined gait according to footfall sequence and can now identify the range of quadrupedal gaits. Walking gaits include diagonal and lateral sequences and can be coupled either diagonally or laterally. Running gaits include trots, paces and gallops, and the latter can be either rotary or transverse.


Hildebrand Gait Plot

In the 1970s Hildebrand studied animal locomotion and established a gait model which maps differences among walking gaits and running gaits. Duty factor is the % of the stride in which the reference hindlimb is in contact with the ground. The walk/run transition occurs at a 50% duty factor. During walking gaits, the hindlimb maintains contact with the substrate through 50% or more of the full stride. Running gaits have duty factors that fall below 50% of the full stride. Gaits are further differentiated by the relative timing of forelimb and hindlimb sequence, known as limb phase. Gait Number is determined as the duration between footfalls of the reference hindlimb to the ipsilateral (same side) forelimb, as a percentage of total stride duration. In sum, footfall patterns can be categorized as walking or running gaits based on Duty Factor, and specific gait types are determined by their Gait Number.

Examine the Hildebrand plot on the right. Its horizontal axis (x-axis) represents Duty Factor (from 0% to 100% of the full stride), while its vertical axis (y-axis) represents Gait Number (also from 0% to 100% of the stride).

The Evolution of Primate Diagonal Sequence Gait: Small Branch Stability

One hypothesis for the evolution of diagonal sequence gait (DS) proposes that DSDC walking evolved in primates in order to provide stability in a small branch, arboreal environment. Specifically, it is hypothesized that DSDC walking allows primates to support themselves with a protracted hindlimb positioned under the body, well supporting the body's center of mass. Once securely supported by an underbody hindlimb, the following forelimb is able to safely assess potential certainty of thin flexible branches (Cartmill, Lemelin, Schmitt 2002).

The goal of this lab section is to test whether or not DSDC is the only kind of walk that confers such stability. Infant primates utilize a broader range of walking gaits than do adult primates. In fact, infant baboons include the three patterns of DSDC, LSDC and LSLC in their gait repertoire. Therefore, infant primates provide us the opportunity to perform naturalistic experiments to investigate the stability hypotheses.
